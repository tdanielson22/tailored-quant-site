An earnest and tad indulgent caraciature of my guiding principles, borrowed from other fields (and how I adapted): 

<span style="font-size: 1em; font-weight: bold;">"Measure twice, cut once"</span>
<!-- which I adapt to quant work as making room to get the question right.** -->

<blockquote>I adapt this idea to quant work as regularly making room to sharpen the question/framing. Correctly answering the wrong question, and not realizing, is a waste of time, an opportunity cost in forgoing a chance to clarify thinking and worst of all risks a bad outcome by adding confidence to a poor decision. </blockquote>

<span style="font-size: 1em; font-weight: bold;">"Do no harm"</span>


<blockquote>The cost vs benefit of quant work is a function of how quickly it sharpens an investor's command of the key tradeoffs, simultaneously supporting and challenging their intuition. Math worth doing organically improves as an investor applies it and finds holes. The value derives from modeling that suits the problem and the decision makers, manifesting as improved understanding and appropriate level of conviction in their decisions. 

<br><br>So the harm can creep in when there is too large a gap within the trio of model design, problem framing and investor understanding. The conversation becomes less nuanced and more binary, ultimately ending in discussions about whether to trust or dismiss the model output. It is not part of the process, but an accessory.

<br><br> In my view, models are not inherently rigorous or flawed. I appreciate the spirit of "garbage in, garbage out" but it is more fun than helpful. I operate through the lens that rigor is entirely in the decision making, not in the analysis. The investor's ability to interpret and apply the information from the math (because it is well designed for the problem and presents relevant and material considerations), alongside other considerations, is the root of the rigor.</blockquote>
